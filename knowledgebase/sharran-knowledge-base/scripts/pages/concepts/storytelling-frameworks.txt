Sharran offers two structures for making a useful point understandable and memorable: insight, story, lesson; and situation, conflict, resolution. Both give an audience more than a chronological list of facts.

Insight, story, lesson begins with the idea the listener should retain. The story makes it concrete, and the final lesson explains what to do with it. His example starts with the claim that good existing clients can produce similar future clients. He then describes elaborate appreciation dinners and a quarter when the dinner did not happen. The reported decline in referrals supplies the tension, and investing in existing clients becomes the takeaway.

The business account is an anecdote. It does not independently establish that missing one dinner caused the change in referrals. Its instructional role is to show how a general insight gains meaning through an event and an explicit conclusion, instead of an unstructured announcement that clients enjoyed dinner.

Situation, conflict, resolution emphasizes why an outcome matters. Sharran introduces an ambition to achieve bestseller status for a book, describes what he considered obstacles in the bestseller system, and concludes with pursuing a Guinness record instead. The audience first understands the initial goal and the barrier, so the final result has context.

His criticisms of bestseller lists and the book-launch numbers remain claims within the example. They are not required as independently proven facts for the communication pattern to be understood.

The distinction between the two structures is emphasis. The first carries a useful idea through a story into a lesson; the second explains a change by setting out the starting situation and the problem that motivated action. Both help an audience follow significance rather than merely hear events.
