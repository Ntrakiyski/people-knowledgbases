Sharran distinguishes being good at a role from wanting to keep doing it. Professional skill and prestigious surroundings can make a mismatch harder to acknowledge, because outward success may conceal dissatisfaction with the actual work.

His banking story turns on a client's business sale. He says the client received approximately $100 million while Goldman earned a much smaller transaction fee, only part of which would reach the individual banker. The comparison initially looks financial, but Sharran interprets it as discovering that he wanted to build and own something rather than remain an adviser to the owner.

That interpretation is personal. In his departure account, he explicitly says people can make money as employees, lawyers, bankers, or entrepreneurs. Neither advising nor operating is inherently the better choice for everyone. The question is whether the position matches the kind of contribution, responsibility, and ownership the person actually wants.

His subsequent operating experience prevents the story from becoming a simple instruction to follow enthusiasm. He describes entering an unfamiliar industry, receiving mentorship, making mistakes, committing personal money, and taking no salary for a period. Finding a better fit did not mean already having the skills to perform it well.

Another video makes a related distinction between the environment and the seat. A demanding peer group may raise standards while the particular role still remains wrong. Improving surroundings cannot by itself resolve that mismatch.

The practical lesson is to examine the work one is becoming excellent at, not only the recognition attached to it. Sharran's warning is against allowing existing competence to become the sole reason to stay in work one dislikes.
