The capital stack answers two connected questions: what funded a transaction, and what claims did those contributions create? Sharran separates money needed to acquire ownership from money supporting ongoing operations. Debt, intermediate financing, and equity are recurring categories, but he explicitly notes that a real transaction need not contain every standard layer.

The funding view helps reconstruct deals hidden by a headline price. In his Inter Miami example, bank borrowing supports a stadium, private-equity capital supports shareholder changes and growth, and principal owners supply the ownership layer. In the Louisiana Purchase account, payment instruments allow the buyer to promise money over time while banks give the seller immediate liquidity at a discount. His Pokémon reconstruction also recognizes creative labor, development capital, rescue funding, and family support outside a neat modern financing diagram. The reported arrangements are his interpretations of those cases.

The investor’s view focuses on repayment order. If a business fails, senior claims are paid before junior claims and equity. A home provides his simplest analogy: the mortgage lender has a claim ahead of the homeowner. Asking where an investment sits in that line makes risk more concrete than merely watching its quoted price.

Priority still does not establish that repayment will happen. Sharran recounts investing $800,000 as debt and $200,000 as equity in a company whose founder subsequently disappeared. The apparent debt protection did not save his investment. His broader system therefore combines position in the stack with understanding the business, investigating the people, examining incentives, and reviewing the financial rationale. A favorable contractual place in line cannot substitute for an enterprise and counterparty capable of honoring the claim.
