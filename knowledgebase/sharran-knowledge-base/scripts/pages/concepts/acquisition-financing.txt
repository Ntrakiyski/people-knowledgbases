Sharran separates the ability to buy a business from already possessing its entire purchase price in cash. His acquisition examples combine different sources of capital and different forms of consideration, so the important questions are who supplies money at closing, what the seller retains, and how obligations are repaid afterward.

Seller financing changes payment timing. Instead of receiving the whole price immediately, the seller accepts payments and effectively acts as lender. Sharran says many of his deals included some version of this arrangement. The buyer may intend to service the obligation from the acquired business's operating cash flow, but that is a repayment plan rather than an elimination of the debt.

Bank or SBA lending supplies another possible source. He presents underwriting as useful because the lender evaluates the business. His descriptions of government-funded loans and zero-out-of-pocket purchases remain his characterizations, not established program rules or assured availability to any buyer.

His property-management account adds seller equity. A seller receives some cash and retains a stake in a larger platform, potentially participating in a later exit. This differs from a seller loan: retained ownership has a different claim on future value from a scheduled repayment obligation.

The Louisiana Purchase narrative introduces assumed liabilities. In his account, taking responsibility for existing obligations formed part of the consideration. A headline price can therefore differ from immediate cash paid directly to the seller. The obligation has changed hands; it has not ceased to exist.

Across these cases, financing is the design that connects an attractive asset with parties able to fund it under workable terms. Low personal cash at closing does not mean a free acquisition. Debt service, transferred liabilities, seller ownership, and the need for the business to perform remain part of the economic purchase.
