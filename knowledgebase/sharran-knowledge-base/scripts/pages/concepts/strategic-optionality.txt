Srivatsaa argues that a valuable company should offer its owner several attractive choices. Selling can be one of them, but building a business that only works if it sells leaves the owner dependent on a buyer, market conditions, and an acceptable offer arriving at the right moment.

## Plan for alternatives to the exit

His alternatives include continuing to own the company, stepping back from daily operations, selling a portion, selling the entire business, or financing it. Instead of asking only what would make a buyer interested, he asks what would need to be true for those alternatives to remain useful. That broader question becomes a business planning exercise.

Continued ownership must therefore be an attractive outcome in its own right. An owner who can comfortably retain the business has a different negotiating position from someone who must dispose of it. In Srivatsaa’s hypothetical acquisition discussion, an offer competes with the seller’s other options rather than merely with another bidder’s price.

## The ability to decline has economic meaning

Optionality changes how urgency is distributed. When selling is the only acceptable outcome, a delayed process or weak market can turn an opportunity into a trap. When the business can keep operating under a workable ownership arrangement, the owner can decline terms that do not compare favorably with continuing to hold it.

Srivatsaa extends the same reasoning to smaller decisions: deliberately doing nothing can be an available option. That is a choice made against alternatives, rather than a failure to notice a decision.

The teaching does not provide a formula for pricing each option or prove that every company can support all of them. Its central standard is resilience across possible futures. A plan with several viable outcomes gives the owner more room to choose than a plan whose success depends on one exit event.
