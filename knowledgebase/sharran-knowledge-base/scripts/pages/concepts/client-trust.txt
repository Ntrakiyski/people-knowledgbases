Sharran treats client trust as something repeatedly earned through choices, availability, and conduct under stress. A completed sale does not finish the work. The relationship’s future value depends on whether the client expects the adviser to act reliably when the next important event occurs.

His long-term-greed anecdote concerns an unintended extra profit on a structured-note transaction. He says the firm gave up the windfall even though the client had received the promised outcome. The lesson is to evaluate the relationship on a longer horizon than the current deal. His predictions about advocacy and future business are part of the story, rather than measured proof of a particular return on the decision.

Responsiveness supplies an everyday signal of the same commitment. Sharran recalls returning calls promptly and answering notifications at unusual hours. He qualifies this by saying it cannot be done for everyone and should prioritize the best clients. The perceived benefit is certainty that help is available, rather than responsiveness being inherently an efficiency technique.

Failure tests whether that certainty is justified. In his trading-error example, a colleague stops, consults counsel, and contacts the client before the problem arrives through a third party. Sharran’s practical rule is to disclose the issue clearly, take responsibility, and explain the response already underway. Defending the past delays the more useful task of limiting damage.

Finally, he extends the relationship beyond one adviser. A one-firm culture mobilizes specialists across internal boundaries, even when the work is outside an individual’s immediate responsibility. Existing internal relationships make that support easier to obtain. The client experiences the resulting organization as a dependable source of help. Across these practices, trust rests on observable behavior and coordinated action rather than an assertion that a strong brand or adviser will never make a mistake.
