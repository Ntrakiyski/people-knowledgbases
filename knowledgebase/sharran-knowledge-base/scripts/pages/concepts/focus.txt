Focus is Sharran’s answer to the mismatch between unlimited possible projects and limited capacity for serious improvement. Doing fewer things allows enough attention, practice, and follow-through for one activity to become effective. The point is not merely avoiding distraction; it is choosing where unusually sustained effort will go.

His operational sequence is more, better, new. First increase an activity that already works. Next improve how it is performed. Only then add a fresh experiment. This order gives an existing business mechanism a chance to produce more value before the owner creates another system to learn, staff, and maintain. His analogy with deeper personal relationships emphasizes that spreading attention changes the quality of engagement, not just the calendar.

The personal version combines an existing strength with repeated effort. Sharran recalls his father narrowing his attention to tennis and academics rather than encouraging equal ambition everywhere. He argues that effort is more controllable than background advantages or external selection decisions. Focus makes that controllable input large enough to matter in the chosen domain.

Elsewhere, he describes deserving as unreasonable effort with reasonable expectations. The athlete who practices intensely and seeks coaching illustrates the standard he wants people to apply to their own goals. This remains his motivational framework, rather than a universal rule that effort guarantees fair outcomes or that people deserve every circumstance they encounter.

Across the examples, focus supplies a constraint on ambition. One cannot apply exceptional intensity to everything at once. Selecting a narrow objective, working the existing opportunity more fully, and observing improvement is different from repeatedly starting over in search of a project that demands less effort. The framework favors depth where there is evidence of potential, while leaving new experiments later in the sequence.
