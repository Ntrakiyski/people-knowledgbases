Srivatsaa offers several complementary ways to turn an emotionally difficult choice into an explicit decision. They address different problems: unclear context, attachment to an existing commitment, endless delay, weak diligence, and uncertainty about when to stop. They work as questions that expose a decision’s structure rather than as a formula that guarantees the right outcome.

## Establish what is actually being decided

His general leadership process begins with context and a precise issue, then accepts the risks and tradeoffs before mapping the next steps. A proposal is incomplete if it describes what the organization gains without identifying what it cannot do after committing. Returning to these questions can also refocus a heated conversation.

The zero based question examines an existing commitment as though its history were absent. Would someone buy the same asset today with cash, choose the same business, or accept the same identity? His example of a founder holding concentrated Google shares asks whether an equivalent cash windfall would deliberately be invested entirely in that stock. The point is to separate the next allocation from sunk cost and familiarity.

## Define the boundaries before acting

Decision deadlines create a time for judgment without demanding instant certainty. In the expensive hire example, the founder keeps the candidate informed, reviews the economics, and decides within a defined window. Reversibility affects that window: Srivatsaa favors more speed when the choice can be undone and more deliberation when it cannot. Whether an opportunity will remain available matters too.

Kill criteria address the other end of the commitment. Writing down a spending boundary and a required result, as in his hypothetical advertising example, establishes evidence that would justify stopping. It is intended to enable committed execution rather than repeated exits at the first discomfort.

For investment diligence, his four goods require vetted people, sound intentions, a credible financial rationale, and appropriate contracts. The sequence reflects his account of losing money despite apparent legal and financial protection. Paperwork alone cannot establish trustworthy operators or coherent incentives. Across these frameworks, a usable decision names its evidence, timing, tradeoffs, and next action.
