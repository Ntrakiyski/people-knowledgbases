# Investment fee drag

> Over a 20-year period, you are paying over a million dollar in just fees.

[It’s Brutal, But If You Want to Get Rich You Need to Watch This](../sources/it-s-brutal-but-if-you-want-to-get-rich-you-need-to-watch-this-DLubmS-kX4Q.md), 0:09:03.

Sharran uses recurring fees to show why a small annual percentage can matter over a long holding period. The cost is not limited to money deducted this year. That money also stops participating in future growth, so an ending-balance comparison includes both charges and forgone compounding.

His repeated illustration begins with $1 million, assumes twenty years of constant 10% annual growth, and compares it with 9% after a one-percentage-point reduction. He describes the difference as more than a million dollars in fees. The useful distinction is that the entire terminal-value gap is not necessarily a literal invoice total; it also reflects returns the deducted money no longer earns. Neither growth rate is promised.

He connects this to his own discomfort while advising clients. Charging for cash management and receiving asset-based compensation made him question how cost related to value delivered. Yet he also says advisers can provide protection, oversight, and useful guidance. His argument is to understand the arrangement and assess it, rather than assume that all professional help lacks value.

Fund expenses deserve the same attention. He recounts comparing QQQ with QQQM as an example of checking ongoing costs for familiar exposure. His stated percentage reduction is a claim in the transcript, not a verified current fee comparison.

For an adviser conversation, he recommends asking how everybody gets paid: the client, the adviser, and the funds. This turns an uncomfortable question about a single charge into an explanation of the entire economic arrangement. Once the payment structure is visible, the investor can compare service, incentives, and retained return more intelligently.
