# After-action review

> what was supposed to happen? Number two, what actually happened? Number three, was there a gap?

[I’ll Delete Your Financial Anxiety, Just Give Me 18 Mins](../sources/i-ll-delete-your-financial-anxiety-just-give-me-18-mins-OcH0864CLPs.md), 0:12:46.

Sharran presents an after-action review as a way to turn an unwanted result into a changed practice. He says he learned the method while working with the US Navy. The review asks what was supposed to happen, what actually happened, whether a gap existed, and what will change next time.

The first two questions separate expectation from observation. Without that separation, a disappointed decision maker can move immediately to defending the original choice or blaming the result. The third question identifies the missing connection, and the fourth requires a different future action rather than a general statement that a lesson was learned.

His hiring example concerns a desirable candidate who accepts less money elsewhere. The company expected a competitive offer and compelling role to win. The review reveals that another employer supplied a workable hybrid schedule for childcare, a logistical issue the company had never asked about.

The resulting change is concrete: ask early whether childcare, commuting, travel, or scheduling would make accepting the role easier. The lesson is not simply to pay more or make a more enthusiastic pitch. It is to discover an important constraint before designing the offer.

This fits his broader concern about the fear of error. Energy spent protecting the decision maker's credibility is energy unavailable for fixing the process. A review redirects the conversation toward the next attempt.

He adds that an expensive mistake should buy a principle, such as a checklist, decision process, or framework. The artifact matters because it gives the organization something reusable. An after-action review is complete in his approach when the insight changes what people will actually do next time.
