# Long Term Investing

> money loves speed and wealth loves time.

[Once I Understood This About Money, My Life Changed](../sources/once-i-understood-this-about-money-my-life-changed-XoWE1QPMFek.md), 0:19:06.

Sharran contrasts making money quickly from a transaction with retaining an asset long enough for time to matter. His property story gives the distinction a concrete form: repeated flips generate sales proceeds, while a growing rental portfolio preserves ownership alongside potential income and appreciation.

He recounts completing roughly 150 flips and compares his result with a friend who progressed from a duplex to a fourplex and then twelve units. In his telling, the friend ended with a larger net worth, an owned home, rental income, and less stress. Sharran credits holding assets through a rising market rather than repeatedly completing another sale.

The comparison changes the question used to judge activity. The number of deals completed and the speed of each payment can make a strategy feel productive. They do not necessarily reveal how much ownership remains afterward or what must be done to earn the next payment.

His formulation that money loves speed and wealth loves time separates two horizons. Speed can matter when recognizing and executing an opportunity. Time matters after a decision when the investor continues to own a productive asset rather than constantly replacing it with the next transaction.

The story is not a controlled performance comparison. Different properties, leverage, operating work, and market conditions are not held constant, and the video does not calculate a common after-cost return series. The friend's outcome therefore cannot prove that holding always beats selling.

What the source does support is a strategic distinction: optimizing immediate cash receipts is different from building a retained asset base. A plan should be judged against the kind of outcome it is actually intended to produce.
