# Financial leverage

> Leverage multiplies everything.

[If You Only Watch One Money Video, Make It This](../sources/if-you-only-watch-one-money-video-make-it-this-OWorFLIbRoo.md), 0:06:27.

Sharran describes leverage as using borrowing to obtain more asset exposure than the investor's own cash would purchase. His central example keeps the property constant and changes how it is financed.

A house bought for $1 million rises to $1.1 million. With an all-cash purchase, the $100,000 appreciation equals 10% of the original cash. With an $800,000 loan and $200,000 contributed by the buyer, the same appreciation equals 50% of that contributed equity. The asset did not grow faster; the gain is being compared with a smaller equity base.

This is an illustration of exposure rather than a complete net-return calculation. Interest, operating expenses, transaction costs, and financing terms are not included. His broader statement that leverage multiplies everything should be read alongside his instruction to understand the associated risk, not as evidence of a free increase in return.

He extends the mechanism to company acquisitions, commercial property, and borrowing against securities. These examples share a separation between equity contributed and assets controlled. They do not establish that the loans have identical terms or that every borrower qualifies.

Collateral is the organizing concept in his account. The house, building, or stock portfolio gives the lender something to claim if obligations are not met. Describing collateral does not make the obligation disappear.

His commercial-property example includes a broad claim about not needing a personal guarantee. The video does not provide contract terms supporting that as a universal rule. Likewise, his tax discussion concerns his characterization of loan proceeds, not the elimination of all financing or transaction consequences.

The durable lesson is to distinguish asset performance from return on contributed equity and to evaluate the borrowing arrangement supporting that relationship.
