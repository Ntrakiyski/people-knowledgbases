# Deal structure

> the cash bought Game [music] Freak's survival, but the terms are what created the conditions

[Pokémon’s $140B Deal Will Make You Rich (Here’s How)](../sources/pok-mon-s-140b-deal-will-make-you-rich-here-s-how-YHigza2n5wU.md), 0:10:15.

Srivatsaa analyzes a deal as a distribution of rights, obligations, timing, and uncertainty. Price matters, but it does not explain who will control the asset, when cash arrives, what must happen before additional compensation is earned, or who absorbs failure. His repeated deal analyses ask what barrier prevents each party from agreeing and which term could address it.

## Solve the constraint behind the negotiation

Different transactions expose different constraints. In the Louisiana Purchase account, banks bridge a seller wanting cash and a buyer lacking immediately available funds. In the Warner narrative, cash certainty, proof of financing, delay compensation, treatment of a breakup payment, and buying the whole business address concerns distinct from the offered price. The transcript is inconsistent about the breakup figure, so the structural lesson is more reliable than an exact numerical reconstruction.

Nonfinancial constraints can be decisive too. In his account of Peter Steinberger joining OpenAI, preserving an independent, open source project matters alongside compensation. The video acknowledges that exact participation and pay terms are unknown. It illustrates how a cash offer can fail when it disregards what the other party values, rather than documenting a complete contract.

## Follow the future rights

His Pokémon analysis distinguishes creation, rescue financing, publishing, distribution, and trademark control. The asserted historical allocations are his claims, but the analytical question is durable: a modest initial contribution can have very different long term value depending on the rights attached to it.

The Messi analysis similarly separates fixed pay, revenue participation, and prospective equity. A contingent payment depends on a different chain of events from a salary payment. Comparing only the advertised totals obscures duration and the performance needed to earn them.

These examples do not establish that a creative structure eliminates cost. Financing creates obligations, contingent upside requires execution, and control can be surrendered through terms that receive little attention at signing. Srivatsaa’s framework makes those exchanges part of the negotiation rather than hidden consequences discovered after agreement.
