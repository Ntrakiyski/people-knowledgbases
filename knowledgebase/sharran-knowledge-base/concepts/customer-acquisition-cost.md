# Customer Acquisition Cost

> your CAC, which is your cost of acquiring a client, is $100

[Exactly How I Built a $3B+ Business in My 30s (3 Pillars)](../sources/exactly-how-i-built-a-3b-business-in-my-30s-3-pillars-7OSLclpwAkI.md), 0:07:03.

Sharran uses customer acquisition cost to connect marketing with the cash required to keep a business growing. Reaching more people is useful only if enough of them become customers at a cost the business can support.

His simple example spends $100 to acquire a client who buys $250 of product. The $100 is acquisition cost; the larger purchase amount creates room to cover that cost and other business expenses. Although he describes the difference as money made, the example does not include delivery costs. The $150 gap should therefore not be treated as demonstrated net profit.

He argues that rising acquisition costs can drain business cash even when demand exists. If it becomes increasingly expensive to obtain another paying customer, more revenue activity may require more cash than expected. This makes acquisition efficiency a business-model concern rather than merely an advertising metric.

Brand enters as a mechanism for lowering that friction. Sharran rejects the assumption that a well-known brand automatically produces an endless stream of inbound messages. He says its advantage also appears when the company pays to advertise: a familiar name can attract attention and trust more readily.

His hypothetical comparison with an Oprah advertisement illustrates the claim. Identical offers may perform differently when the audience already recognizes and trusts one seller. The example is explanatory, not a reported experiment with measured costs.

The operational lesson is to evaluate brand and paid marketing together. Awareness can support conversion rather than replace promotion. At the same time, a recognizable name does not remove the need to examine acquisition spending, customer revenue, and the costs of fulfilling the promise.
