# Value Proposition

> what is one thing if I took away that you would not want to work with us anymore.

[Exactly How I Built a $3B+ Business in My 30s (3 Pillars)](../sources/exactly-how-i-built-a-3b-business-in-my-30s-3-pillars-7OSLclpwAkI.md), 0:03:40.

Sharran defines a useful value proposition as a concrete outcome a customer can understand, value, and repeat to someone else. It replaces a vague promise to create more value with an explicit reason to choose or remain with the business.

His brokerage example begins with customer research rather than a slogan. He says he asked existing agents why they stayed and which removed benefit would make them leave. Their answers challenged his assumptions: the platform was valuable because it saved time. That became a proposition of returning one day each week, expressed as fifty-two days over a year.

The phrasing makes the exchange imaginable. An agent can consider what another day would allow: more selling, fewer administrative tasks, or more personal time. The company can then evaluate new services against the same promise. Sharran says it kept this focus throughout his ownership, so the proposition operated as a filter for delivery as well as marketing.

He uses a different message for another brokerage platform: multiple income streams. The contrast matters because a proposition should reflect the particular customer benefit a business can deliver, rather than copying the wording that succeeded elsewhere.

His familiar advertising examples serve the same purpose. A specified amount of time exchanged for a possible insurance saving is more legible than a general claim of excellence. The specificity explains the customer's side of the transaction.

The business-growth figures and his claims about what drove them are personal accounts, not independent causal evidence. What the examples substantiate is a method: investigate why customers choose the service, identify the benefit they would miss, express it clearly, and align the business around delivering it.
