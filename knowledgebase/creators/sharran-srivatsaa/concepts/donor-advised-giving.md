# Donor-advised giving

> Think of it like a giving account.

[Millionaire Advice I'm Giving My Kids I Wish I Knew in My 20s](../sources/millionaire-advice-i-m-giving-my-kids-i-wish-i-knew-in-my-20s-ISQkh8bu6EU.md), 0:22:21.

Srivatsaa presents a donor advised fund as a family giving account that separates the time of contributing assets from the later selection of charities. In his parenting framework, its purpose is both financial organization and repeated practice in generosity.

He compares three approaches: giving cash directly to a charity, contributing cash to a donor advised fund, and contributing appreciated stock. In his account, the fund can receive a contribution now while grants to selected charities occur later. The appreciated stock example is intended to show why the asset contributed can matter alongside the donation amount.

His tax descriptions are simplified claims in the video. He says appreciated stock can support a deduction while avoiding tax on its embedded gain, and compares the fund to a private foundation with less paperwork. The transcript does not explain qualification limits, deduction rules, account restrictions, or the legal differences between those structures. It therefore supports the speaker’s timing and giving framework rather than an unconditional promise of a particular deduction.

## Make giving an activity children can practice

Srivatsaa says his children can view the balance, choose a recipient, and initiate a grant. The discussion afterward is central: why this charity, what will it do with the money, and why does the support matter?

This turns generosity from an abstract instruction into an observable decision about resources and consequences. He places it after earning, learning skills, accessing capital, and acquiring ownership so that stewardship includes using wealth for others. In that account, generational wealth is a way of thinking and acting that a family repeatedly teaches, rather than simply a balance passed to children.
