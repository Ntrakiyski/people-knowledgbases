# A-player hiring

> a business model that is challenging and lucrative

[If You Only Watch One Business Video, Make It This](../sources/if-you-only-watch-one-business-video-make-it-this-wXhUtIvnr9U.md), 0:02:03.

Sharran describes recruiting strong performers as a learnable operating skill. His starting point is the business model: it must offer both challenge and attractive economics. Hiring an exceptional person into work that cannot support or retain them leaves the underlying problem unsolved.

He imagines a reinforcing cycle. A capable hire contributes value, the business produces more profit, and those economics allow further investment in people. He contrasts this with choosing an easy, low-margin model and then discovering it cannot afford the people required to improve it. His suggestion that a modest pay premium can purchase vastly greater output is a heuristic from his teaching, not a measured rule for every role.

His interview approach looks for three kinds of evidence. First, ask the candidate to teach something. The subject can be simple; the useful signal is whether the person can organize an idea, think clearly, and communicate it. Second, ask how they prepared for the interview. Sharran treats specific preparation as evidence of care that is difficult to manufacture convincingly after the fact.

Third, listen to the candidate’s questions about contribution. He values interest in strategic priorities, expected results, and what meaningful success would look like over time. His preference is for discussion of value creation rather than compensation being the only topic. This is his interview lens, not proof that negotiating pay indicates poor capability.

The framework joins selection with retention. Communication and preparation help assess the person, while challenge and sustainable economics help make the job worth staying in. A high standard for candidates therefore requires a corresponding standard for the opportunity the company offers them.
