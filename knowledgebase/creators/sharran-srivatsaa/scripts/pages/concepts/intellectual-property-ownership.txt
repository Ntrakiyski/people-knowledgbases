Sharran uses Pokémon to show why ownership of creative rights can matter long after the original product is released. The asset in his account is not only a game. It can support cards, merchandise, animation, licensing, and later products that reuse the same characters and world.

That creates a different negotiation horizon from receiving cash for one task. An ownership interest may participate in future uses that do not yet exist. He compares this with his account of George Lucas accepting less immediate compensation while retaining merchandising and sequel rights. The examples illustrate trading present certainty for exposure to future value.

His Pokémon analysis also separates kinds of rights. He describes shared economic participation among Game Freak, Creatures, and Nintendo, while assigning distinct creative, publishing, distribution, and trademark control. The historical allocations and legal claims are his account, not independently established ownership research.

The distinction prevents a common oversimplification: owning a percentage of something does not automatically mean controlling every decision or channel through which it reaches customers. In his narrative, Nintendo's distribution position and claimed trademark control matter alongside its equity interest. Creative labor, rescue capital, and access to a platform purchase different strategic roles.

He also notes that the initial financing amounts are undisclosed. The later success of the franchise therefore does not reveal a complete original valuation or prove that any rescue investment in distressed creative work is attractive.

The durable lesson concerns the object of negotiation. Price matters, but so do the future revenue uses covered, who authorizes them, and who controls access to the audience. A one-time payment and a continuing claim on intellectual property are different economic positions.
